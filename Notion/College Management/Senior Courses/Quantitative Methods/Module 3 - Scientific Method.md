## The Empirical Cycle (Hypothetico-Deductive Approach) 🔄

The **Empirical Cycle** is a systematic and rigorous process for generating and testing hypotheses against empirical data. It characterizes the **Hypothetico-Deductive (H-D) Method** of science, ensuring research is constantly informed by observation and subject to empirical scrutiny.

***

### The Five Phases of the Empirical Cycle

The cycle moves between specific observations and general rules, constantly refining knowledge.

#### 1. Observation Phase
* **Action:** An observation sparks the idea for a new research hypothesis. This can be an intriguing pattern, an unexpected event, or a research finding.
* **Source:** Can be personal, shared, imaginary, or, most commonly, derived from previous systematic research.
* **Goal:** To observe a **relation in one or more specific instances** (e.g., "My friends and I have horrible mothers-in-law").

#### 2. Induction Phase
* **Action:** The relation observed in specific instances is transformed into a **general rule or hypothesis**.
* **Reasoning:** **Inductive Reasoning** is used—inferring that a statement true in specific cases is true in all cases.
* **Goal:** To formulate a **general, testable hypothesis** (e.g., "All mothers-in-law are horrible"). This hypothesis is **not necessarily true**; it must be tested.

#### 3. Deduction Phase
* **Action:** The general rule (hypothesis) is used to **deduce an explicit expectation or prediction** about new, specific instances.
* **Reasoning:** **Deductive Reasoning** is used—if the general rule is true, a specific outcome *must* follow.
* **Setup:** This phase requires **specification of the research setup**, including:
    * Definitions of concepts.
    * Measurement instruments and procedures.
    * The sample from which new data will be collected.
* **Goal:** To transform the hypothesis into a **precise, testable prediction** (e.g., "If I ask ten colleagues to rate their mother-in-law, all ten will choose 'horrible'").

#### 4. Testing Phase
* **Action:** The hypothesis is tested by **collecting new empirical data** and **comparing it to the prediction**.
* **Analysis:** Requires the use of **descriptive statistics** (to summarize data) and **inferential statistics** (to decide if the prediction was correct).
* **Goal:** To determine if the prediction is **confirmed or disconfirmed** (e.g., "Two out of ten colleagues rated their mother-in-law as 'neutral,' refuting the prediction that all ten would rate 'horrible'").

#### 5. Evaluation Phase
* **Action:** The results are **interpreted in terms of the original hypothesis**.
* **Confirmed Prediction:** Provides only **provisional support** for the hypothesis; it is **never definitively proven** (acknowledging the Problem of Induction).
* **Refuted Prediction:**
    * Often leads to a search for **plausible explanations** related to the **research setup** (e.g., flawed sampling or measurement), in which case the hypothesis is **preserved** and investigated again with a better design.
    * Can lead to an **adjustment** of the hypothesis based on the results.
    * Leads to **rejection and discarding** of the hypothesis only in rare cases.
* **The Cycle:** The new empirical data collected in the Testing Phase often serves as **new specific observations** for the next Observation Phase, restarting the cycle. This continuous process allows science to **hone in on the best hypotheses** and build understanding.

## Interpreting Research Results: Confirmation vs. Disconfirmation 🧐

In the empirical cycle, the final steps involve interpreting whether new data supports or contradicts the prediction derived from a hypothesis. The nature of this interpretation—especially in social sciences—is crucial due to the **Problem of Induction** and methodological complexity.

***

### I. Interpreting Confirmation (Provisional Support)

* **Scenario:** The empirical results match the specific prediction (e.g., all 10 colleagues rate their mother-in-law as "horrible").
* **Conclusion:** The prediction is confirmed, but the hypothesis is **never proven**.
* **Reasoning (Problem of Induction):** Confirmation is **never conclusive**. It is always **easily conceivable** that future observations (e.g., repeating the study with a new sample) will contradict the findings (e.g., finding someone who adores their mother-in-law).
* **Result:** The hypothesis is considered **provisionally supported**.
* **Strength:** The more consistent support a hypothesis receives from **different studies** and varied research setups, the **more credence** (plausibility) it is afforded.

> **Key takeaway:** No scientific empirical statement can ever be **proven once and for all**. The best outcome is overwhelming, provisional support.

***

### II. Interpreting Disconfirmation (Refutation)

* **Scenario:** The empirical results contradict the specific prediction (e.g., 8 rate "horrible," but 2 rate "neutral").
* **Logical Implication:** Logically, contradictory findings **should lead to the rejection** of the hypothesis (e.g., finding a black swan conclusively rejects the "All swans are white" hypothesis).
* **Practice in Social Sciences:** Outright rejection is **rare** because there are often **plausible alternative explanations** for the failure to confirm, especially concerning the **research setup** (the Duhem-Quine Thesis).

#### A. Methodological Rejection (Preserving the Hypothesis)
* Disconfirmation is often attributed to **methodological issues** (auxiliary assumptions about the study design), rather than the hypothesis itself.
* **Plausible Explanations:**
    * The **measurement instrument** was inappropriate (e.g., the rating scale was flawed).
    * **Procedural error** (e.g., forgetting to tell colleagues responses were confidential, making them reluctant to choose the negative category).
    * **Relevant background variables** weren't controlled for.
* **Result:** The researcher **rejects the auxiliary methodological assumptions** and **preserves the original hypothesis**, then investigates it again with a better research setup.

#### B. Hypothesis Modification (Slight Adjustment)
* Disconfirmation can lead to a **slight modification** of the hypothesis to incorporate the new results.
* *Example:* If the 8 "horrible" ratings were from women and the 2 "neutral" ratings were from men, the hypothesis might be modified to: "**All mothers-in-law are horrible, but only to their daughters-in-law.**"
* **Result:** This is, strictly speaking, a rejection of the original, more general hypothesis. The new hypothesis is **less general and therefore less strong**, but it is **not a radical rejection**.

> **Note:** **Outright rejection** or radical adjustment is very rare in the social sciences. Progress is typically made in **small increments**, as researchers continuously refine hypotheses and research designs through the empirical cycle.



## Evaluating Scientific Studies: Reliability and Validity ✅

After conducting a study, two main criteria—**Reliability** and **Validity**—are used to decide how convincing the findings are, especially since confirmation is never proof and disconfirmation isn't always cause for rejection.

***

### 1. Reliability (Consistency)
* **Definition:** A research finding is **reliable** if the study is actually **repeated** (replicated) and produces **consistent results**.
* **Relation to Replicability:** Reliability is the *outcome* of **replicability** (the principle that independent researchers can repeat the study).

***

### 2. Validity (Accuracy/Truth)
* **Definition:** A study is **valid** if its conclusion about the hypothesized relationship between properties **accurately reflects reality**. In short, the conclusion based on the results is **true**.
* **General Question:** In the hypothesis "loneliness causes depression," if giving the elderly a cat decreases depression, is the conclusion valid? Does it truly support the hypothesized cause-and-effect?

There are three specific types of validity used to answer this question:

#### A. Construct Validity (Measurement Accuracy)
* **Definition:** The degree to which the properties or **constructs** in the hypothesis are **accurately measured and manipulated** by the methods used.
* **Prerequisite:** Construct validity is an **important prerequisite** for achieving high internal and external validity.
* **Threats (Low Construct Validity):**
    * **Measuring the wrong effect:** The depression questionnaire accidentally measures "social exclusion" instead of "depression."
    * **Manipulating the wrong cause:** Giving the cat increased "feelings of responsibility" and "self-worth" but **didn't actually decrease loneliness**.
* **Result of Low Validity:** If methods don't measure or manipulate what they intend, the study may *seem* to support the hypothesis when, in reality, different constructs were involved.

#### B. Internal Validity (Causal Accuracy)
* **Definition:** Relevant when describing a **causal relationship**. A study is **internally valid** if the **observed effect is actually due to the hypothesized cause** (and not something else).
* **Threats (Alternative Explanations):** Internal validity is **low** if a plausible **alternative explanation** exists for the observed effect.
    * *Example:* Depression decreased not because of the cat's company (decreased loneliness), but because:
        * **Change in Season:** The study started in winter and ended in spring.
        * **Increased Exercise:** The increased physical activity from cleaning the litter box caused the mood improvement.

#### C. External Validity (Generalizability)
* **Definition:** A study is **externally valid** if the hypothesized relationship supported by the findings also **holds true in other settings and other groups**. It is also called **generalizability**.
* **Threats (Lack of Generalization):**
    * **To other people/groups:** Will the cat intervention work for **teenagers** or **middle-aged people**?
    * **To other settings/contexts:** Will the effect be the same for people from **different cultures**?
    * **To other conditions:** Will a **dog** be as effective as a cat?
* **Confidence:** Confidence in external validity is increased by studying diverse groups and conditions.

***

### Summary of Validity Types

| Type of Validity | Core Question                                                                           | Threats / Relates to...                                                                                |
| :--------------- | :-------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- | :----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| **Construct**    | Do the methods (measures/manipulations) accurately **reflect the intended properties**? | Measuring or manipulating the wrong variable (e.g., measuring social exclusion instead of depression). |
| **Internal**     | Is the hypothesized cause the **actual cause** for the observed effect?                 | **Alternative explanations** or confounding variables (e.g., seasonal change).                         |
| **External**     | Does the hypothesized relation **hold true in other settings** and for other groups?    | **Generalizability** (e.g., can results from the elderly be applied to teenagers?).                    |

## Causal Relationships: Hume's and Mill's Criteria ⛓️

Identifying a **causal relationship** (knowing what causes an effect) is crucial because it allows us to predict, influence, and better understand phenomena. The criteria used to establish causation were first outlined by **David Hume** and formalized by **John Stuart Mill**.

***

### The Four Essential Criteria for Causation

A hypothesized relationship between a **cause** (C) and an **effect** (E) must satisfy these four criteria:

#### 1. Connection (Proximity)
* **Principle:** There must be a traceable link between the cause and effect. They must be connected in terms of **space, time, or person**.
* **Example:** In the hypothesis "loneliness causes depression," the decrease in loneliness and the decrease in depression occur within the **same person** and close in **time**.

#### 2. Temporal Precedence
* **Principle:** The **cause must precede the effect** in time.
* **Example:** The decrease in the cause (loneliness, via getting a cat) **must happen before** the effect (decrease in depression) is observed. This is often controlled by the researcher.

#### 3. Consistent Concomitance (Covariation)
* **Principle:** The cause and effect must **occur together consistently** (covary).
    * When the **cause is present**, the effect should be present.
    * When the **cause is absent**, the effect should be absent.
    * If the cause influences the effect to a certain **degree**, the effect should show a **corresponding strength** or weakness.
* **Example:** **Less loneliness** (cause) should consistently go together with **lower depression** (effect). A comparison group that does **not** get a cat (cause is absent) should show **no change** in depression (effect is absent).

#### 4. Exclusion of Alternative Explanations
* **Principle:** All other plausible causes for the observed effect must be **ruled out**.
* **Difficulty:** This is the most difficult criterion to satisfy.
* **Threat to Validity:** Alternative explanations form **threats to the internal validity** of a study.
* **Example:** If depression decreases, but the researcher hasn't controlled for the possibility that the **increased physical activity** (litter box cleaning) caused the improvement, then the causal claim is weak. Methodology focuses on research designs (like randomized control groups) to **minimize these threats**.

***

### Causation vs. Correlation

* **Causation Requires Correlation:** For a causal link to exist, the cause and effect **must covary** (Criteria #3).
* **Correlation Does Not Imply Causation:** Observing that two variables occur together consistently does **not** prove one causes the other.
* **Alternative Interpretations of Correlation:**
    * **Reverse Causality:** The effect might be the cause (e.g., Aggressive children *seek out* violent video games, rather than the games causing aggression).
    * **Confounding Variables:** A third, unmeasured variable might influence both (e.g., Children allowed to play violent games are **less closely supervised**, and this lack of supervision, not the game, leads to disinhibited aggressive behavior).

> **Rule to Remember:** **Correlation does not imply causation.**
