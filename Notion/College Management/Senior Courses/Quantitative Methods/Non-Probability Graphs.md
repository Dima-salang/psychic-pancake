## 1. Introduction

In statistics, data representation is as important as data collection.
**Non-probability graphs** are **visual tools used to represent data that isn’t based on chance or probability**. Instead, they are primarily descriptive: they allow us to *see patterns, compare categories, and communicate information effectively*.

**Why use graphs?**

* Humans process visuals faster than numbers.
* Trends, comparisons, and proportions are easier to see graphically.
* Graphs form the basis for further decision-making in **business, engineering, and science**.

**Core Learning Outcomes:**

1. Identify and construct different types of non-probability graphs.
2. Interpret data from graphs.
3. Compare graphs and choose the most appropriate type for a dataset.
4. Apply graphing skills to real-life contexts such as **survey results, school performance, or weather data**.

## 2. Types of Non-Probability Graphs

Let’s go through each type carefully.

---

### 2.1 Pareto Chart

**Definition (from source):**
A Pareto Chart is a graph used to represent a frequency distribution for categorical (nominal-level) data, where **frequencies are displayed by vertical bars arranged from highest to lowest**.

**Key Features:**

* Categories are ordered by frequency, not alphabetically.
* Often accompanied by a **cumulative percentage line**, showing the “Pareto principle” (80/20 rule).

**Why important?**

* Helps identify the **most significant categories** contributing to a problem.
* Widely used in **quality control** (e.g., identifying top causes of product defects).

**Example:**
If product defects are due to: Scratches (40), Cracks (25), Color Fade (20), Mislabel (15):

* Arrange bars in descending order: Scratches → Cracks → Color Fade → Mislabel.
* The first few bars usually account for the majority of cases.

---

### 2.2 Bar Chart

**Definition (from source):**
A Bar Chart is a graph similar to a histogram but used for categorical (nominal-level) data.

* Bases of rectangles are arbitrary intervals whose centers represent the categories.
* Heights represent frequency.

**Clarification:**

* Unlike histograms (which are for continuous, numerical data), **bar charts are for discrete or categorical data**.
* Bars can be vertical or horizontal.

**Use Cases:**

* Comparing survey responses (“Yes, No, Maybe”).
* Displaying frequency of favorite sports among students.

---

### 2.3 Pie Chart (Circle Graph)

**Definition (from source):**
A circle divided into slices representing relative frequencies (percentages) of categories.

* Data must be **categorical or nominal**.
* Each slice = $\frac{\text{Category Frequency}}{\text{Total Frequency}} \times 360^\circ$.

**Advantages:**

* Easy to see proportions of a whole.
* Good for **market share, budget allocation, election results**.

**Limitations:**

* Hard to compare categories with similar sizes.
* Not effective when there are too many categories (>6).

---

### 2.4 Scatter Plot

**Definition (from source):**
A graph used to examine possible relationships between two numerical variables.

* One variable is plotted on the **x-axis**, the other on the **y-axis**.
* Each point = one observation.

**Interpretation:**

* Look for **patterns, trends, and correlations** (positive, negative, none).
* Useful for regression analysis and predictive modeling.

**Example:**
Hours studied (x) vs. exam score (y) may show a positive trend.

---

### 2.5 Time Series Graph

**Definition (from source):**
A graph that represents data over a **specific period of time**.

* Usually plotted as a **line graph**.
* Shows trends, seasonality, cycles, or sudden changes.

**Applications:**

* Stock market prices.
* Weather temperature trends.
* Monthly sales or production output.

**Key Insight:**
Time series analysis is the foundation of **forecasting models** (e.g., ARIMA in statistics, LSTMs in machine learning).

---

### 2.6 Pictograph

**Definition (from source):**
A graph that uses **pictures or icons** arranged in rows or columns to represent quantities.

**Example:**
If 1 apple icon = 10 students, and we show 🍎🍎🍎, that represents 30 students.

**Advantages:**

* Simple and visually appealing for younger audiences.
* Effective in non-technical communication (e.g., infographics).

**Limitations:**

* Can be misleading if icons are not scaled properly.
* Not suitable for precise analysis.

---

## 3. Comparing Graph Types

* **Bar Chart vs. Histogram:** Bar chart = categorical, histogram = continuous.
* **Pareto vs. Bar Chart:** Pareto orders categories by frequency, bar chart does not necessarily.
* **Pie Chart vs. Bar Chart:** Pie = proportion of whole, Bar = direct frequency comparison.
* **Scatter Plot vs. Time Series:** Scatter = relationship between two variables, Time Series = trend over time.
* **Pictograph vs. All Others:** Best for simple communication, not for rigorous analysis.

---

## 4. Drawing Conclusions and Real-Life Applications

* **Survey Results:** Bar chart or pie chart for categorical responses.
* **Weather Data:** Time series graph for temperature trends.
* **School Performance:** Pareto chart to identify major reasons for low grades.
* **Market Analysis:** Pie chart for market share, bar chart for product sales.
* **Engineering & Quality Control:** Pareto chart to find key defect causes.

---

## 5. Importance of Non-Probability Graphs

* They **simplify complex data** into digestible visuals.
* They allow us to **spot patterns quickly** that raw numbers may hide.
* They are the **first step** in more advanced analysis (statistics, data science, AI).
* In professional settings, graphs are critical in **presentations, reports, and decision-making**.

---

## 6. Summary

* **Pareto Chart:** Categorical data ordered by frequency.
* **Bar Chart:** Compares frequencies of categories.
* **Pie Chart:** Shows proportions of a whole.
* **Scatter Plot:** Shows relationships between two numerical variables.
* **Time Series Graph:** Shows data trends over time.
* **Pictograph:** Uses icons for simple quantity representation.

**Core Principle:**
Choose the graph type that **best matches the nature of the data and the story you want to tell.**
