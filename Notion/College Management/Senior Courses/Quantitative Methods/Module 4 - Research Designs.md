## Experimental Research: The True Experiment (RCT)

True experiments, also known as **Randomized Controlled Trials (RCTs)**, are the most effective research designs for establishing **causal relationships** and maximizing **internal validity**. They provide the strongest defense against **alternative explanations** for observed effects.

***

### Essential Ingredients of a True Experiment

A true experiment is defined by three necessary components:

#### 1. Manipulation (Controlling the Cause)
* **Action:** The researcher actively **controls the cause** (the independent variable).
* **Benefit:** By creating the situation where the cause is present, manipulation ensures that the cause **precedes the effect**, thus eliminating the threat of **ambiguous temporal precedence** (Criteria #2 for causation).

#### 2. Comparison (The Control Group)
* **Action:** The manipulated condition (**experimental group**) is compared to a situation where the cause is **absent** (**control group**).
* **Benefit:** Comparison shows that the effect **does not occur naturally** or due to other factors (like **maturation**—natural change over time). This is crucial for satisfying the **Consistent Concomitance** (Covariation) criterion (Criteria #3).
    * *Example:* Comparing children encouraged to play a violent game to a control group denied access to violent imagery.

#### 3. Random Assignment (Excluding Alternatives)
* **Action:** Participants are assigned to the experimental or control conditions **randomly** (e.g., by flipping a coin). 
* **Benefit:** **On average**, randomization ensures that there is **no systematic difference** between the groups on any variable other than the independent variable (cause). This applies to variables like age, gender, previous aggression, etc.
* **Goal:** Maximizes **internal validity** by ensuring that any observed difference in the effect (dependent variable) can be confidently attributed to the manipulation, thereby fulfilling the **Exclusion of Alternative Explanations** criterion (Criteria #4).

***

### The Role of Replication

* **Limitation of Randomization:** In any single study, randomization might fail purely by chance (e.g., accidentally assigning more aggressive children or more girls to one group).
* **Necessity of Replication:** **Replication** (repeating the study) is required to ensure that the results are **consistent**. Consistency across multiple studies provides confidence that the initial randomization was effective and that the effect is reliable, confirming the causal claim.
* **Note:** Replication is generally **not considered an *ingredient*** of a single true experiment, but it is **required to validate** its findings and ensure the randomization worked as intended.

## Experimental Research: Factorial Designs 📊

**Factorial designs** are a type of experimental research that investigates **two or more independent variables (factors)** simultaneously within a single study. They are highly efficient and allow researchers to examine complex relationships.

***

### 1. Basic Structure of a Factorial Design

* **Factors (Independent Variables - IVs):** There are two or more IVs being manipulated.
* **Levels:** Each factor must have at least two conditions or levels.
* **Design Setup:** Factors are **crossed**—meaning every level of one factor is combined with every level of every other factor. This creates a matrix of **cells** (conditions).
* **Goal:** To ensure all possible combinations of the IVs are represented, with enough participants in each cell.
* **Dependent Variable (DV):** There is one primary outcome variable (e.g., number of migraine attacks).

#### Example: Two-Factor Design (Dosage $\times$ Gender)
| | Dosage: Low ($L_1$) | Dosage: Medium ($L_2$) | Dosage: High ($L_3$) |
| :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: |
| **Gender: Men** ($G_1$) | Cell 1 (Men, Low) | Cell 2 (Men, Medium) | Cell 3 (Men, High) |
| **Gender: Women** ($G_2$) | Cell 4 (Women, Low) | Cell 5 (Women, Medium) | Cell 6 (Women, High) |
*This design has $2 \times 3 = 6$ conditions/cells.*

***

### 2. Types of Effects Investigated

Factorial designs allow researchers to identify two primary categories of effects:

#### A. Main Effects
* **Definition:** The effect of **one independent variable** on the dependent variable, **averaging across the levels of all other factors**.
* **Example from above:**
    1.  **Main Effect of Dosage:** Is a higher dosage generally more effective at reducing migraine attacks, regardless of gender?
    2.  **Main Effect of Gender:** Do women generally suffer more migraine attacks than men, regardless of the dosage they receive?

#### B. Interaction Effects
* **Definition:** The **combined effect** of two or more factors on the dependent variable. An interaction occurs when the effect of one factor **depends on the level of another factor**.
* **Two-Way Interaction:** The combined effect of two factors (e.g., Dosage $\times$ Gender).
    * *Example:* Is a higher dosage more effective for women compared to men (or vice versa)?
* **Higher-Order Interactions (Three-Way, etc.):** The combined effect of three or more factors (e.g., Dosage $\times$ Gender $\times$ Diet).
    * *Example:* Does the dosage effect for women **and** the dosage effect for men change differently depending on whether they follow a "No Fun Diet" or a "Normal Diet"?

***

### 3. Complexity and Utility

* **Increased Complexity:** As the number of factors or levels increases, the number of conditions grows rapidly ($2 \times 3 \times 2 = 12$ conditions if Diet is added), making the design and interpretation of high-order interactions very complicated.
* **Usefulness:** Factorial designs are **very useful** because they go beyond simple main effects to provide a nuanced understanding of **when** and **for whom** an intervention or cause is most effective (i.e., they identify **moderating factors**).
* **Incomplete Designs:** There are more complex designs (e.g., incomplete designs) where not all combinations of factor levels are present, but these are typically saved for advanced study.

## Research Designs: Between vs. Within Subjects Factors 🧪

Research design choices dictate how participants are exposed to the independent variable (IV) and are key to efficiency and internal validity. The main distinction is between **Between-Subjects** and **Within-Subjects** factors.

***

### 1. Between-Subjects Design (Between Factor)

* **Definition:** Different groups of participants are exposed to the different **levels** (conditions) of the independent variable.
* **IV Type:** **Between-Subjects Factor** (or simply **Between Factor**).
* **Example:** One group of patients receives a **low dosage** of a drug, and a different group of patients receives a **high dosage**.
* **Key Feature:** Each participant experiences **only one** level of the IV.

***

### 2. Within-Subjects Design (Within Factor)

* **Definition:** Each participant is exposed to **all levels** (conditions) of the independent variable.
* **IV Type:** **Within-Subjects Factor** (or simply **Within Factor**). This is often synonymous with a **Repeated Measures Design**.
* **Example:** The same patient receives the **low dosage** for one week, the **medium dosage** for a second week, and the **high dosage** for a third week.
* **Advantages:**
    * **Statistical Efficiency:** Reduces the influence of individual differences, as each person acts as their own control.
    * **Practical Efficiency:** Requires fewer total participants (e.g., 40 people for 3 weeks vs. 120 people for 1 week).

***

### 3. Combining Factors (Mixed Factorial Designs)

Factors can be combined in a factorial design:

* **Within $\times$ Between Design (Mixed Design):** This combines factors where some are manipulated within-subjects and others are between-subjects.
    * *Example:* Investigating **Dosage** (Within Factor: all participants receive low, medium, and high) and **Gender** (Between Factor: participants can only be male or female).
    * *Result:* A group of men experiences all three doses, and a group of women experiences all three doses, allowing for the study of **interaction effects** (e.g., if the drug is more effective at high doses for women than for men).

***

### 4. Related Terminology

| Term | Definition | Timeframe | Typical Focus |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Within-Subjects Design** | A design consisting of **within factors only**; the same participants are measured after exposure to each condition. | Shorter | Manipulated IVs and causal claims. |
| **Repeated Measures Design** | Used as a synonym for a Within-Subjects Design, but also refers to **complex designs with at least one within factor** (Mixed Factorial). | Shorter | Manipulated IVs and causal claims. |
| **Longitudinal Design** | Studies that measure the same variables repeatedly over a **long period of time** (months, years, decades). | Long | Primarily **Correlational studies** (no IV manipulation), though experimental studies are included if long-term manipulation is possible. |

* **Key Distinction:** **Repeated Measures** implies repeated measurement of the dependent variable following a manipulated IV; **Longitudinal** implies repeated measurement over an extended duration, usually for correlational study. 


## True Experiments: Manipulation and Control 🔬

**Manipulation** is one of the three essential ingredients of a true experiment (alongside comparison and random assignment). It refers to the researcher's active **control over the independent variable (IV)** and their efforts to **control external variables** to maximize internal validity.

***

### 1. Manipulation of the Independent Variable (IV)

Manipulation ensures the researcher determines the value or **level** of the IV a participant experiences.

#### A. Levels, Conditions, and Groups
* **Levels:** The different ways or degrees to which the IV is present.
    * *Example:* For the IV **Violent Imagery**, the levels could be: 2 hours of gaming, 4 hours of gaming, and 0 hours of gaming.
* **Conditions/Groups:** Synonyms for levels, referring to the distinct settings or treatments participants experience.
* **Control Condition (Control Group):** The level where the independent variable is **absent** (e.g., the group that does not play the violent video game).
* **Experimental Variable:** A variable that is **fully controlled and manipulated** by the researcher.

#### B. Manipulable vs. Non-Manipulable Variables
* **Individual Differences Variables:** Properties intrinsic to the participant that **cannot** be manipulated (e.g., age, gender, ethnicity). These remain **between-subjects factors**.
* **Manipulable Constructs:** Some variables that seem like individual differences (e.g., self-esteem, mood) **can be manipulated** through experimental procedures.
    * *Example:* Manipulating **Self-Esteem** by providing bogus intelligence test scores (high score = boosted self-esteem; low score = decreased self-esteem).

#### C. Manipulation Check
* **Definition:** A measurement taken to ensure that the intended level of the IV was **actually experienced** by the participants and had the intended psychological effect.
* *Example:* After giving bogus test scores, the researcher measures participants' self-esteem to confirm it was boosted or decreased as planned.
* **Procedure Note:** A manipulation check should be performed **after** measuring the dependent variable to avoid giving away the purpose of the experiment, which could bias the participants' behavior.

***

### 2. Control of External Variables (The Ceteris Paribus Principle)

Experimental control involves keeping variables of disinterest constant to rule out alternative explanations.

#### A. Ceteris Paribus Principle
* **Meaning:** "All other things equal."
* **Goal:** The ideal is for each experimental condition to be **entirely identical** except for the manipulated independent variable. If a difference is found on the dependent variable, it must be caused by the IV.

#### B. Control Variables
* **Definition:** External variables that are **held constant** (the same) across all conditions.
    * *Example:* In a video game study, the researcher controls the **wall color** and **lighting** to ensure these external variables don't affect aggression differently across groups.
* **Challenge in Social Sciences:** It's extremely difficult to control all relevant social and psychological aspects of a situation, making the job of the social scientist much harder than that of the physicist.
* **Controlling Individual Differences Variables:** Since individual differences (like age or educational level) cannot be physically held constant, researchers rely on **randomization** and **matching** to ensure these variables do not systematically differ between conditions (to be discussed in detail later).


## Lab vs. Field Research: Internal and Ecological Validity 🧪

Rigorous investigation of causal hypotheses requires **manipulation** of the IV and **control** of extraneous variables. This need for control dictates the setting of the research, leading to a key trade-off between **Internal Validity** and **Ecological Validity**.

***

### 1. Lab Research

**Lab research** is conducted in an environment entirely under the researcher's control, which maximizes internal validity.

#### A. Characteristics of the Lab
* **Definition:** In social science, a lab is simply a highly **controlled environment** (e.g., a quiet room with a computer, a video-equipped play area) that allows the researcher to hold all extraneous variables constant.
* **Primary Goal:** To maximize **internal validity** by ensuring that any observed effect is due solely to the manipulated independent variable (satisfying the *ceteris paribus* principle).

#### B. The Validity Trade-Off
* **High Internal Validity:** Achieved through manipulation and control.
* **Potential Low Ecological Validity:** Ecological validity (or **mundane realism**) refers to how closely the research setting and procedures **approximate real-world phenomena**. Lab settings are often criticized for being artificial (e.g., a computer-simulated salary negotiation).

#### C. Experimental Realism
* **Defense Against Low Ecological Validity:** Low ecological validity is not necessarily fatal if the study maintains **experimental realism**.
* **Definition of Experimental Realism:** The experimental translation of a phenomenon might be highly artificial, but it must be **sufficient within the lab setting** to adequately measure or manipulate the intended constructs.
    * *Example:* Telling a vulnerable participant they are "confused" and not "the brightest bulb" is an artificial manipulation, but it may **effectively induce short-term low self-confidence** in the lab (high construct validity) and still demonstrate the causal relationship to negotiating behavior.

***

### 2. Field Research

**Field research** is conducted outside the lab, in an uncontrolled, natural environment.

* **Setting:** A public area, private residence, workplace, or any natural setting ("in the wild").
* **Advantages (Maximized Validity):** Naturally lends itself to the observation of real, natural behavior, maximizing **ecological validity** and potentially **external validity** (generalizability).
* **Field Experiments:** Field research can be **experimental** (e.g., manipulating feedback given to job candidates before their actual salary negotiation).
* **Disadvantages (Threatened Validity):**
    * It's **impossible to control** all extraneous and confounding variables, which lowers **internal validity**.
    * Ethical issues are more complex when manipulating variables in real-world settings (e.g., giving false feedback to job candidates).

***

### 3. Complementary Approaches

| Research Type | Setting | Control | Validity Maximized | Primary Risk |
| :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: |
| **Lab Study** | Highly Controlled | High | Internal Validity | Low Ecological/External Validity (Artificiality) |
| **Field Study** | Natural Environment | Low | Ecological/External Validity | Low Internal Validity (Alternative Explanations) |

* **Conclusion:** Lab and field studies **complement each other**. A causal relationship established with high internal validity in a lab can be tested for generalizability (external validity) in the field, and vice versa.
## Maximizing Internal Validity: Randomization and Control 🎲

**Randomization (Random Assignment)** is the key ingredient in a true experiment that allows researchers to satisfy the **Exclusion of Alternative Explanations** criterion, thereby maximizing **internal validity**.

***

### 1. The Principle of Random Assignment

* **Mechanism:** Participants are assigned to experimental conditions (e.g., experimental group vs. control group) using a process based entirely on chance (e.g., coin flip, random number generator).
* **Goal:** To ensure that, on average, there is **no systematic difference** between the groups on *any* characteristic—known or unknown, relevant or irrelevant (e.g., age, gender, prior aggression, shoe size).
* **The Law of Large Numbers:** Randomization relies on the statistical principle that over a large number of assignments, any individual characteristic will be distributed **equally** across all groups.

#### **Example: Violent Imagery Study**
If children are randomly assigned to a "Violent Game" group or a "Control" group, we expect:
* About **half** of the naturally aggressive children to end up in the Violent Game group.
* About **half** of the non-aggressive children to end up in the Violent Game group.
* The groups are balanced on aggression *before* the manipulation begins.

***

### 2. Randomization Failure and Solutions

#### A. Randomization Failure
* **Definition:** A chance occurrence, especially in **small groups**, where randomization fails to distribute key characteristics (like gender or aggression) equally, leading to a **systematic difference** between groups.
* **Problem:** The systematic difference provides a plausible **alternative explanation** for the effect, threatening internal validity.

#### B. Solutions (Restricted Randomization)
To guarantee balancing on key variables, researchers can use **restricted randomization procedures**:

| Method | Goal | Mechanism |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Blocking** | Ensures **equal or near-equal group sizes**. | Participants are paired into blocks (usually of size 2). One member of the block is randomly assigned, and the other is automatically assigned to the opposite condition. |
| **Stratified Random Assignment** | Ensures **equal representation** of a specific characteristic (e.g., gender, age). | Participants are first grouped by the characteristic (**strata**, e.g., all girls are grouped). Then, blocking or simple randomization is performed *within* each stratum to ensure equal numbers of that characteristic are assigned to each condition. |

* **Limitation of Stratification:** It becomes overly complicated when trying to stratify on more than two or three characteristics, and it's impossible to control for the endless number of subject characteristics.

#### C. Randomization Check
* **Procedure:** Researchers measure relevant **background or control variables** (e.g., pre-manipulation aggression scores) and check after randomization to see if the groups are statistically equivalent on these variables.

***

### 3. Randomization in Within-Subjects Designs

In **within-subjects designs** (repeated measures), all subjects experience all conditions, so group balancing is not needed. However, randomization is still crucial to prevent sequence effects:

* **Threat:** If all subjects experience the conditions in the same order (e.g., Dose Low $\rightarrow$ Dose Medium $\rightarrow$ Dose High), any change in the dependent variable could be due to **maturation, practice, or habituation** (carryover effects).
* **Solution: Counterbalancing**
    * The **order** in which subjects are exposed to the conditions is **randomized**.
    * Subjects are assigned to one out of all possible orderings of the conditions, often using blocking to ensure no one ordering is overrepresented. 
## Common True Experimental Designs 🧪

A **true experiment** maximizes internal validity through **manipulation**, **comparison**, and **random assignment**. These core elements can be combined into several common experimental designs.

***

### 1. Two-Group Design (Post-Test Only)

* **Structure:** Participants are **randomly assigned** to one of two conditions.
    * Condition 1: **Experimental Group** (Cause is Present/Specific Level).
    * Condition 2: **Control Group** (Cause is Absent/Different Level).
* **Measurement:** The **Dependent Variable (DV) is measured *after*** exposure to the Independent Variable (IV).
* **Assumption:** Due to random assignment, the groups are assumed to be similar in all respects, including their preexisting position on the DV. Any post-test difference is attributed to the IV.
* **Example:** Randomly assigning boys to either a male math teacher or a female math teacher for a month, then comparing their final math performance.

***

### 2. Two-Group Pretest-Post-Test Design

* **Structure:** Adds a **pretest** of the DV *before* exposure to the IV.
* **Measurement:** DV is measured at **Time 1 (Pretest)** and **Time 2 (Post-test)**.
* **Benefits:**
    * **Checks Randomization:** Allows the researcher to check whether the groups were truly **equivalent** on the DV before the manipulation (useful for small samples where randomization can fail).
    * **Measures Maturation/Change:** Allows the researcher to compare the **size of the change** (increase or decrease in scores) in both the experimental and control groups, separating natural improvement (maturation) from the IV's specific effect.
* **Risk:** The **Pretest Sensitization Effect**. The pretest might:
    * Cause a **practice effect** (leading to artificially higher post-test scores).
    * **Alert participants to the study's purpose**, potentially biasing their post-test scores (especially if the effect is stronger in one group).

***

### 3. Solomon Four-Group Design

* **Structure:** A combination of the **Two-Group Design** and the **Two-Group Pretest-Post-Test Design**. 
* **Four Groups (All Randomly Assigned):**
    1.  Experimental Group with Pretest ($\text{Pretest } \rightarrow \text{Treatment } \rightarrow \text{Post-test}$)
    2.  Control Group with Pretest ($\text{Pretest } \rightarrow \text{No Treatment } \rightarrow \text{Post-test}$)
    3.  Experimental Group without Pretest ($\text{No Pretest } \rightarrow \text{Treatment } \rightarrow \text{Post-test}$)
    4.  Control Group without Pretest ($\text{No Pretest } \rightarrow \text{No Treatment } \rightarrow \text{Post-test}$)
* **Benefit:** Allows the researcher to **isolate and measure the effect of the pretest** itself. By comparing the results of the two pretest groups to the two non-pretest groups, the researcher can determine if the pretest interacted with the IV or independently obscured the true effect.

***

### 4. Repeated Measures Design (Within-Subjects Factor)

* **Structure:** All participants are exposed to **all levels (conditions)** of the independent variable.
* **Random Assignment Focus:** Participants are **randomly assigned to the order** in which they experience the conditions, rather than to the conditions themselves.
* **Control Mechanism:** **Counterbalancing** (randomizing the order of condition exposure) is essential to eliminate threats from sequence effects (e.g., practice, fatigue, or carryover effects from one condition to the next).
* **Example:** Half of the boys get the female teacher first, then the male teacher. The other half gets the male teacher first, then the female teacher. Performance is measured after each month.


## Experimental Control without Randomization: Matching and Undermatching 🤝

In many research situations, true experiments with random assignment are impossible due to **pragmatic** (cost, logistics), **ethical**, or **intrinsic** reasons (when the IV is an **individual differences variable** like sex or age). When random assignment is impossible, the threat of **selection** (systematic pre-existing differences between groups) to internal validity is high.

***

### 1. Matching to Mitigate Selection Threat

* **Strategy:** **Matching** is used to create comparable conditions by finding groups of participants that are equivalent on specific, relevant background variables.
* **Mechanism:** Participants are paired or grouped based on a characteristic (e.g., age, educational level), and those that don't match are often discarded.
* **Benefit:** It **rules out specific, known alternative explanations** (e.g., if men and women are matched on age, age cannot explain any difference in political conservatism).

***

### 2. The Danger of Undermatching

Matching is a useful technique, but it can create a new threat to internal validity if done incorrectly: **Undermatching**. This risk occurs when:

1.  The matching variable is measured with **measurement error**.
2.  The matching variable is **related to the variables of interest** (IV and DV).

To understand undermatching, we must first understand its cause: **Regression to the Mean**.

#### A. Regression to the Mean
* **Definition:** The statistical phenomenon where an extreme score on one measurement is likely to be **closer to the group mean** on a subsequent measurement, simply due to **measurement error**.
* **Mechanism:** Any single extreme score (very high or very low) is likely a combination of the person's true score and a large random measurement error (overestimation or underestimation). On a second test, the measurement error is likely to be smaller, pulling the observed score back toward the average.
* *Example:* A person with a true IQ of 110 scores 132 (a high, uncommon score) due to error. Their next score is likely to be lower, closer to 110 or the population mean of 100.

#### B. How Undermatching Occurs (The Matching Paradox)
Undermatching is the distorting effect of regression to the mean when matching on a variable measured with error.

1.  **Selection of Extreme Subgroups:** The researcher intentionally selects groups that are **naturally different** on the IV (e.g., **Disadvantaged** vs. **Advantaged** children) but then selects a sample that is **equal on the matching variable** (Pretest Cognitive Ability).
    * To achieve equal pretest scores, the researcher must select:
        * **Disadvantaged Children** with **relatively high** scores (overestimators for their group).
        * **Advantaged Children** with **relatively low** scores (underestimators for their group).
2.  **Regression Effect:** When the post-test is given (without intervention):
    * The **Disadvantaged** group's high scores **regress down** toward their group's naturally lower mean.
    * The **Advantaged** group's low scores **regress up** toward their group's naturally higher mean.
3.  **Result (Undermatching):** Without any intervention, the two groups will appear to **diverge** on the post-test, with the Disadvantaged group scoring lower.

* **Consequence:** This regression effect can mask a beneficial intervention or, worse, lead to the inaccurate conclusion that the intervention had a **detrimental effect** on the group that regressed downward.
* **Avoidance:** This threat can be avoided by matching on variables that are measured **without significant error** (e.g., age, sex, educational level).


## Quasi-Experimental Designs (QEDs) 🚧

**Quasi-experimental designs (QEDs)** look and feel like true experiments but fundamentally **lack the key feature of random assignment**. They are generally used when random assignment is impossible due to **pragmatic, ethical, or intrinsic** (individual differences) reasons.

***

### 1. Core Characteristics

| Feature | True Experiment | Quasi-Experiment | Implication for Internal Validity |
| :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: |
| **Random Assignment** | **Present** | **Absent** | **Selection Threat** is always present. |
| **Manipulation** | Usually Present | May or May Not be Present | Causal order may be ambiguous. |
| **Control** | Maximized | As Much as Possible | Requires additional methodological efforts (e.g., pretests) to rule out alternatives. |
| **Goal** | Establish Causal Relationship | Establish Causal Relationship | The causal claim is weaker than in a true experiment. |

* **Natural Experiments:** QEDs where the IV is a naturally occurring event or characteristic (e.g., war experience, gender) are often referred to as natural experiments.
* **QED vs. Correlational Study:** QEDs are similar to correlational studies but specifically **examine causal hypotheses** and exert maximum effort to **control extraneous variables**.

***

### 2. Common Quasi-Experimental Designs

#### A. Static Group Comparison Design
* **Structure:** Similar to the Two-Group Design, but with **nonrandom assignment**.
    * Group 1 (Non-Equivalent Experimental Group) $\rightarrow$ Treatment $\rightarrow$ Post-test
    * Group 2 (Non-Equivalent Control Group) $\rightarrow$ No Treatment $\rightarrow$ Post-test
* **Threats:** The **selection threat** is constant. It is impossible to know if post-test differences are due to the treatment or **pre-existing differences** between the non-equivalent groups (e.g., more permissive parents selecting the violent game group).

#### B. Pretest-Post-Test Non-Equivalent Control Group Design
* **Structure:** Adds a **pretest** to the static group comparison design.
    * Group 1 (Non-Equivalent Exp.) $\rightarrow$ Pretest $\rightarrow$ Treatment $\rightarrow$ Post-test
    * Group 2 (Non-Equivalent Ctrl.) $\rightarrow$ Pretest $\rightarrow$ No Treatment $\rightarrow$ Post-test
* **Benefit:** Allows the researcher to **check for pre-existing differences** on the dependent variable. If pretest scores are equal, the causal order is less ambiguous.
* **Remaining Threat:** Even if pretest scores are equal, the groups may still differ on other **unmeasured** variables (selection threat).

#### C. One-Group Pretest-Post-Test Design
* **Structure:** Similar to the experimental version but **lacks a control group**.
    * Group 1 $\rightarrow$ Pretest $\rightarrow$ Treatment $\rightarrow$ Post-test
* **Benefit:** Only establishes **temporal precedence** (the cause came first).
* **Threats:** Highly susceptible to threats from **maturation, history, and testing effects**.

#### D. Interrupted Time-Series Design
* **Structure:** Improves on the one-group design by including **multiple measurements** of the DV both before and after the treatment (**interruption**). 
* **Measurement:** $\text{O}_1, \text{O}_2, \text{O}_3 \rightarrow \text{Treatment} \rightarrow \text{O}_4, \text{O}_5, \text{O}_6$
* **Benefit:** Allows assessment of the **natural change trend** (baseline) in the DV, which helps rule out **gradual maturation** and **history** if a **sudden jump** in the DV occurs precisely at the point of treatment.

#### E. Replicated Interrupted Time-Series Design
* **Structure:** Adds a second group or a second treatment time point to rule out history threats.
    * **Non-Equivalent Control Group:** Adds a second group that receives no treatment but is measured at the same time points. If the control group shows the same pattern of change, a **history threat** is likely present.
    * **Treatment at Different Time Points:** A second group receives the exact same treatment but at a **later time**. If the effect appears only *after* the treatment is administered to the second group, confidence in the causal claim is increased.


## Correlational Research Designs: Measuring Associations 📏

**Correlational designs** are distinct from experimental and quasi-experimental designs because they either **do not manipulate any independent variable (IV)** or they do not even specify a causal hypothesis. They focus purely on **measuring** variables as they exist naturally.

***

### Causal Caution in Correlational Studies

* **No Manipulation/Selection:** Researchers only measure variables; they do not control the cause.
* **Weak Internal Validity:** Any causal inference must be made with **extreme caution** because:
    * **Temporal Precedence** is very difficult to establish (Criteria \#2 for causation).
    * The **Selection Threat** (pre-existing differences) is always present.
    * The **Exclusion of Alternative Explanations** is nearly impossible to satisfy.

***

### Standard Correlational Designs

Correlational studies are often categorized based on the dimensions of **individuals** and **time**.

#### 1. Cross-Sectional Design
* **Structure:** A **cross-section** (large group) of a population is measured at **one specific point in time**.
* **Purpose:** To investigate the **association between variables** in a way that accurately describes the larger population.
* **IV/Groups:** Participants are **not selected beforehand** based on IV levels; groups may be compared *within* the overall sample (e.g., comparing current smokers to current non-smokers in a large survey).
* **Note:** The term **Survey Study** is sometimes used synonymously, but this is misleading as it refers only to the measurement method, not the research setup.

| Dimension | Individuals | Time |
| :---: | :---: | :---: |
| **Cross-Sectional** | Many (Large Group) | One Point |

#### 2. Time-Series Design
* **Structure:** Focuses on **one person** being measured at **several points in time**, often with many measurement moments in quick succession (fixed intervals).
* **Context:** Used interchangeably with **Longitudinal Design** in some fields, but this can cause confusion.
* **Note:** The term Time-Series is also used for a specific **quasi-experimental design** where a group is measured before and after an intervention. In a purely correlational context, if it refers to a single individual measured repeatedly, the term **N=1 study** is often used, especially in experimental contexts.

| Dimension | Individuals | Time |
| :---: | :---: | :---: |
| **Time-Series (Purely Correlational)** | One (Individual) | Many Points (Fixed Intervals) |

#### 3. Panel Design (Time Series Cross-Sectional Design)
* **Structure:** Follows **more than one individual** (a group of the same individuals) over a **longer period of time**, with measurements taken at several points. This combines the "many individuals" of a cross-sectional study with the "many points in time" of a time-series study.
* **Purpose:** To observe how associations or variables change over time within the same group, without an intervention.
* **Cohort Design:** A special case of a panel design where all participants share a common starting point (e.g., same age, started school/job at the same time).

| Dimension | Individuals | Time |
| :---: | :---: | :---: |
| **Panel Design** | Many (Same Group) | Several Points (Long Period) |

## Special Types of Research Designs 💡

Beyond the standard experimental, quasi-experimental, and correlational designs, researchers in specific fields use several specialized study types for unique purposes.

***

### 1. Case Studies (Single Subject Research)

* **Definition:** Studies that focus intensely on **one person or one small group**.
* **Quantitative Focus:** When quantitatively oriented (using numerical data), these are often referred to as **Single-Subject, N=1, or Time-Series Research** (measuring one person repeatedly).
* **Qualitative Focus:** More commonly associated with qualitative research aimed at **generating hypotheses** rather than testing them.
* **Negative Case Analysis:** A qualitative technique that fits the hypothetico-deductive approach.
    * **Mechanism:** The researcher actively searches for a single case that provides **contradictory evidence** (a counter-example) against a hypothesis.
    * **Logic:** While confirmation is provisional and requires much evidence, theoretically, **one solid negative case** can reject an "all or none" hypothesis.

***

### 2. Evaluation Research and Intervention Studies

These forms of applied research investigate the effectiveness of real-world solutions but differ in scope and setting.

| Study Type | Focus/Scope | Primary Setting | Purpose |
| :---: | :---: | :---: | :---: |
| **Evaluation Research** | **Policy or Program** with a broad scope (e.g., educational reform, community health initiatives). | Real World, Societal/Educational Context | **Summative:** Assess *outcome* (was it effective?). **Formative:** Assess *process* (how does it work?). Often non-experimental due to ethical/practical issues. |
| **Intervention Studies** | Effectiveness of a **method aimed at treating individual problems** (e.g., a specific therapy, remedial teaching). | Clinical or Developmental Settings | Investigate specific treatments. Usually **experimental** (e.g., using RCTs to test a therapy's efficacy). |

***

### 3. Validation Research (Psychometrics/Sociometrics)

* **Definition:** Research aimed specifically at assessing the **quality and accuracy of a measurement instrument** (e.g., surveys, questionnaires, tests designed to measure attitudes, traits, or abilities).
* **Mechanism (Key Analyses):**
    1.  **Internal Consistency:** Statistical analysis to ensure that responses to the various questions within the instrument **all tap into the same underlying property** (are highly correlated).
    2.  **Expected Patterns of Association:** Checking if the instrument's scores show the expected patterns of association with other, theoretically related (or unrelated) properties.
* **Importance:** Essential in fields like **Psychometrics** and **Sociometrics** to ensure that tools used to measure abstract concepts are reliable and valid before being used in larger studies.
